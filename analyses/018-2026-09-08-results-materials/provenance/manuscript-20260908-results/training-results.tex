\subsection{Quality--sparsity trade-offs}
\label{sec:quality-sparsity-results}

The 14M results occupy distinct quality--sparsity regimes
(Figure~\ref{fig:quality-sparsity-overview}). Local OL1 at $h$ can improve
validation loss while introducing a modest amount of model-wide sparsity:
at $\lambda=0.5$, A1-H-OL1 reaches $3.768\%$ sparsity at loss 5.1102,
compared with A0's loss of 5.2086. A0 post-hoc clipping reaches a similar
sparsity level, $3.832\%$ at target $p=0.3$, but with loss 5.3619. Thus,
the trained representation and evaluation-only clipping offer different
quality costs even near the same aggregate sparsity.

Broader interventions reach a different regime. A7-OL1 at $\kappa=0.5$
attains $27.483\%$ model-wide sparsity at loss 5.8294, accepting a quality
cost relative to A0. This comparison also changes computational reach:
the A0 clipping reference acts at $a,m,h,z$, whereas A7 additionally gates
the internal attention operands. At 14M, these site sets have analytic reach
ceilings of $12.833\%$ and $29.952\%$, respectively. The overview therefore
motivates separating the effects of pressure and gate placement; a single
ranking of complete recipes cannot explain their differences. Full endpoints
and clipping trajectories are given in Appendix~\ref{app:complete-results}.

\subsection{Paired intervention effects}
\label{sec:paired-results}

Pressure's additional value depends on the gate setting and target set
(Figure~\ref{fig:paired-intervention-effects}). Replacing GELU with ReLU
alone increases model-wide sparsity by 2.714 percentage points (pp), but
raises loss by 0.0611. Adding local pressure improves loss relative to this
ReLU control. Changing naive L1 to OL1 lowers loss at three of the four
matched weights, but reverses that improvement at $\lambda=1$; the update
rule is not uniformly better across the sweep.

For A4, adding OL1 improves both loss and sparsity at $\kappa=0$ and 0.01.
At larger thresholds it adds sparsity at increasing quality cost. At
$\kappa=0.5$, the increment is 2.498 pp for $+0.3783$ loss. The corresponding
A7 pressure addition yields 12.096 pp for $+0.1265$ loss, while at
$\kappa=0.1$ it yields 1.372 pp for a much smaller $+0.0008$ loss change.
Broader gates also contribute without pressure: A7 minus A4 at
$\kappa=0.5$ adds 5.171 pp for $+0.0432$ loss.

These contrasts establish that pressure's response is conditional on the
executed recipe. Comparing its response across A4 and A7 also changes the
pressure objective and its normalization, so the difference cannot be
assigned to gate placement alone. The next comparison examines how those
complete recipes redistribute activation mass.

\subsection{Reshaping activation distributions}
\label{sec:activation-reshaping}

The recipes produce different distributions even when both make FFN
activations highly sparse (Figure~\ref{fig:activation-reshaping}). At
$\kappa=0.5$, A4-OL1 places $99.31\%$ of pooled FFN elements exactly at
zero, compared with $93.64\%$ for A7-OL1. Their attention distributions
differ more sharply: A4-OL1 has a narrow nonzero peak and only $0.22\%$
exact zeros, whereas A7-OL1 has $95.60\%$ exact zeros and nonzero density
outside the symmetric threshold gap. The omitted zero masses are reported
in Table~\ref{tab:density-mass}; a narrow density peak is not itself a large
exact-zero probability.

A4 does not directly gate or pressure $q,k,v$, yet their distributions
change substantially. This is a nonlocal response under the complete A4-OL1
intervention, consistent with spillover. The comparison with A0 does not
isolate pressure's contribution from the other training changes. A7 directly
intervenes at these attention sites, so its reshaping there has a different
interpretation.

The operation counts show why this distinction matters model-wide. At the
same threshold, $QK$ and $PV$ contribute 16.77 sparsity points under A7-OL1,
versus 0.06 under A4-OL1 (Appendix~\ref{app:operation-results}). Several
projection contributions are smaller under A7-OL1, but its attention
contribution more than offsets them. Greater model-wide sparsity can
therefore coexist with less pooled FFN sparsity. This 14M diagnostic
connects the observed distributions to counted work; the scale comparison
tests the recipe ordering rather than replicating this distribution analysis.

\subsection{Transfer across model sizes}
\label{sec:scale-results}

The high-threshold A7-OL1 advantage persists at all three model sizes
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale-transfer}). At $\kappa=0.5$, A7-OL1 has both lower
loss and greater model-wide sparsity than A4-OL1 at 14M, 70M and 410M
(Table~\ref{tab:cross-scale-recipe-contrasts}). The zero-threshold ordering
does not transfer unchanged: A4-OL1 is better on both axes at 14M and 70M,
whereas A7-OL1 is better at 410M. Persistence concerns a specific recipe
relationship, not the entire threshold response.

Raw sparsity must also be read against the changing workload. The A7 reach
ceiling rises from $29.95\%$ to $49.42\%$ and $87.25\%$ as the dense LM
head accounts for a smaller share of counted products. The bottom row uses
the same A7 denominator for every curve within a size:
$U_{\mathrm{arch}}=\Smodel/\Smax(\mathrm{A7})$. Because A7 reaches all
counted block operations, this ratio equals the block zero-product fraction
$\Sblock$. It removes the dense-head share while retaining the different
weights of operations within the blocks.

The larger cohorts compare complete A4-OL1 and A7-OL1 recipes; they lack the
no-pressure A4/A7 controls needed to repeat the isolated pressure additions.
They also share a token budget rather than tokens per parameter, and 410M
uses a lower peak learning rate. The evidence thus supports one-seed,
fixed-budget recipe transfer. It does not establish a scaling law or show
that the distribution response has the same cause at every size.

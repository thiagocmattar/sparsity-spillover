\subsection{Realizing sparsity with specialized inference kernels}
\label{sec:kernel-autoresearch}

Realizing activation sparsity requires kernels matched to the model's shapes,
nonlinearities and hardware. The released TwELL kernels~\citep{cetin2026sparser}
accelerated their supported workload, but the unchanged small-width primitive
failed numerical qualification for Pythia-14M. A shape- and sign-adapted
descendant, P0, qualifies on only four of our 30 checkpoints, with geometric
mean speedup $0.8910\times$ on that subset (Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-results}).
These are compatibility results for the tested shapes and implementation;
the four-checkpoint mean is not a fair aggregate comparator for our full cohort.

To make this specialization practical within an architecture study, we use
a human-guided coding-agent loop to propose, test and refine implementations.
Execution and profiling feedback provide a basis for this
approach~\citep{ouyang2025kernelbench,luo2026agentic}.
We replay 42 eligible proposals from the Codex search against native PyTorch
scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) in CUDA graphs on one RTX5090.
The workload is BF16, batch-one, uncached 2,048-token inference with full
vocabulary logits. Qualification requires logits and loss to agree with the
native reference within specified tolerances on complete validation.
The recorded agent default is \emph{gpt-6-astra}, without a verified served
model for every request; this single trajectory does not measure agent
productivity against human-only development.

The selected implementation K050 qualifies on all 30 checkpoints in the
14M cohort, with geometric mean native-relative speedup $1.2340\times$.
The matched search retrospective reaches $1.7830\times$ on its fixed
A7-OL1, $\kappa=0.5$ checkpoint; Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-results}
retains the search history. Across the final cohort, canonical FP16
model-wide sparsity is associated with BF16 native-relative speedup
(descriptive OLS $R^2=0.8167$). Topology, weights, quality and execution
overhead vary across these checkpoints, so this association alone cannot
attribute the gain to skipping zero products.

Ablations distinguish fusion from the contribution of sparse paths.
With all sparse paths disabled, the fused implementation already achieves
$1.1829\times$ geometric mean speedup over native execution
(Figure~\ref{fig:kernel-autoresearch}). Relative to this control, K050 is
$1.311\times$ as fast at the search checkpoint and $1.043\times$ as fast
geometrically across the cohort, helping 14 of 30 checkpoints.
These incremental factors derive from separately measured native-relative
timings; they do not credit fusion as a sparsity gain.

Attention skipping is unprofitable in this workload:
disabling it improves every checkpoint, raising the mean to $1.2506\times$.
The attention kernel can skip a matrix-multiply instruction when either
complete operand fragment is zero. At the search checkpoint, it bypasses
$58.0\%$ of eligible $QK$ and $67.2\%$ of $PV$ instructions, yet remains
slower than executing those paths densely. Partially zero fragments can
still require an instruction, and detection and control have a cost.
Scalar zero opportunity alone is therefore insufficient to select a
profitable execution path.

The incremental sparse-path factor has a weaker association with canonical
FP16 sparsity than native-relative speedup does ($R^2=0.5037$ versus
$0.8167$). Removing the searched checkpoint gives $R^2=0.4080$ for the
incremental factor; removing its entire A7-OL1 family gives $0.4910$.
Appendix~\ref{app:runtime-sensitivity} reports all leave-family and
within-family checks. These descriptive comparisons retain the FP16/BF16
mismatch and are not prospective prediction tests.

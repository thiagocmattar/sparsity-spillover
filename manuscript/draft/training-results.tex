\subsection{Extensive ablations at 14M}
% Evidence: analyses/033-2026-09-21-14m-latency-quality-frontier/observations/006-threshold-tables.md.
% Values: data/frontier.json, generated by Analysis033/01_plot.py; ceilings: Table~\ref{tab:interventions}.

We compare intervention breadth and strength at 14M, separating aggressive thresholding ($\kappa=0.5$) from moderate settings ($\kappa=0.05,0.1$).
\textbf{Broader coverage increases attainable sparsity without consistently reducing latency.}
At $\kappa=0.5$, $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ more than doubles the sparsity of $T_4/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ (27.48\% versus 12.71\%), yet takes 0.473 versus 0.459\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:14m-aggressive}). Within $T_7$, broadening pressure from $P_h$ to $P_{\mathrm{all}}$ adds 10.82 percentage points of sparsity with nearly unchanged latency, while loss rises from 5.732 to 5.829. All seven aggressive recipes reduce latency relative to Base but increase loss.

\textbf{Moderate thresholding with local pressure offers a better quality--latency balance.}
At both moderate thresholds, $P_h$ gives lower loss and latency than $P_{\mathrm{all}}$ under both $T_4$ and $T_7$ (Table~\ref{tab:14m-moderate}). At $\kappa=0.05$, $T_4/P_h$ reaches 0.562\,ms with loss 5.196, versus Base's 0.652\,ms and 5.209. Narrowing thresholding to $T_2/P_h$ improves loss further to 5.134, but takes 0.590\,ms at 5.00\% sparsity. Thus, the preferred breadth depends on the quality cost one accepts; maximizing model-wide sparsity alone does not select the best execution trade-off. \subsection{Spillover: Local pressure induces nonlocal sparsity}
\label{sec:paired-results}
% Evidence: Analysis021/pressure-scope/observations/O017-layer-sparsity-style.md;
% plot_layer_sparsity.py and data/layer-sparsity-review.json.
% Geometry: Analysis024/observations/039-ol1-appendix.md; 29_ol1_appendix.py and data/ol1-appendix.json.

\textbf{Pressure at $h$ increases sparsity at $z$ under both $T_4$ and $T_7$.}
At $\kappa=0.05$, $P_h$ raises $h$ sparsity from 74--85\% to 94--99\% across layers (Figure~\ref{fig:pressure-layer-sparsity}). Sparsity at the unpressured but thresholded $z$ site also rises in every layer: from 71--85\% to 83--92\% under $T_4$, and from 70--87\% to 84--94\% under $T_7$. This spillover is therefore not confined to four-site thresholding, and suggests that $z$ is responsive to the changes induced by pressure at $h$ without requiring direct pressure.

\textbf{Broad pressure changes this response differently under $T_4$ and $T_7$.}
With $T_4/P_{\mathrm{all}}$, $z$ reaches 99.74--99.85\% sparsity in layers 2--4. Such near-total sparsity could restrict the input-dependent attention signal passed through the output projection to the residual stream, potentially contributing to the higher loss (5.490 versus 5.196 for $P_h$). This is a possible explanation, not an isolated cause of the quality degradation. Under $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$, instead, $q,k,v$ become sparser overall but remain at only 3--13\% across sites and layers, while $h$ and $z$ become less sparse than under $P_h$ in every layer.

\textbf{OL1 geometry helps explain why broader pressure need not produce more sparsity at $h,z$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ol1-geometry} shows larger opposing components and pre-cap correction norms under $P_{\mathrm{all}}$, especially for $T_7$. At $\kappa=0.05$, the median component removed by projection is 66.84\% of the adaptive task-direction norm for $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ and 1.12\% for $T_4/P_{\mathrm{all}}$, versus 0.17\% and 0.15\% under their respective $P_h$ recipes. The corresponding median pre-cap norm ratios are 46.00 and 0.28, versus 0.08 and 0.10. Thus, $T_4/P_h$ and $T_7/P_h$ have similar geometry; extending pressure beyond $h$ demands larger corrections and removes a larger conflicting component. Projection and norm capping may consequently limit the pressure reaching $h$ under $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$. Together with the spillover response, these patterns support greater susceptibility to sparsification at $h,z$, although the joint changes in pressure targets do not isolate the contribution of each added site.

\subsection{When sparsity translates to speedup}
\label{sec:activation-reshaping}
\label{sec:kernel-autoresearch}
% Controlled effects: Run037 observations/001-operation-latency.md;
% Analysis024/25_build_operation_latency_table.py; Run039 observations/001-am-port-verdict.md.

We next test whether the sites that respond most strongly also save execution time. Table~\ref{tab:kernel-mechanisms} isolates each skipping path at 14M by disabling it while keeping weights, gates and the other paths fixed. It reports \emph{bypass} (the percentage of omitted matrix instructions) and conditional saved time for $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ at $\kappa=0.5$.
\textbf{The measurable savings come from $h$ and $z$}: $150.1\,\mu$s and $29.0\,\mu$s, respectively.
The $a,m$ and joint $q,k$ effects remain unresolved against process variation, whereas $PV$ skipping adds $6.2\,\mu$s. These effects are conditional on the other paths remaining enabled and are not additive allocations of total savings.

Sparsity reduces latency when avoided work exceeds the overhead of detecting and skipping it.
At $h,z$, the sparse kernel checks $8\times16$ activation tiles
before loading their associated weights, so empty tiles avoid both weight reads and matrix multiplication. Applying the same strategy at $a$ and $m$ instead increased full-model latency by $17.2\%$ and $27.4\%$ on this checkpoint, with unchanged outputs.
Only $11.12\%$ of $a$'s $8\times16$ tiles were empty, and none at $m$, so
most groups still required matrix multiplication and the additional overhead was not justified.

Attention follows FlashAttention's tiled computation~\citep{dao2022flashattention}, but our zero checks occur after loading the operands. Skipping a matrix instruction therefore avoids arithmetic without avoiding those reads or the masking and softmax work. Softmax also turns zero scores into nonzero probabilities, so $q,k$ sparsity does not automatically propagate to $PV$.
Enabling $QK/PV$ skipping together raises
full-model latency from 0.514 to 0.521\,ms despite substantial bypass.
Thus, for this checkpoint and implementation, attention's arithmetic savings
do not offset its overhead, while $h/z$ benefit from avoiding weight reads
as well as computation.

\textbf{The sparsity and execution diagnostics converge on $h,z$.}
This motivates $T_2/P_h$: pressure remains local to $h$, while thresholding is restricted to $h,z$. At 14M and $\kappa=0.1$, it reaches $5.17\%$ model-wide sparsity against a $5.35\%$ ceiling, with loss $5.151$ versus Base's $5.209$, and latency 0.573\,ms versus native Base's 0.655\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:14m-quality-latency}; Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-results}). The narrower recipe preserves quality while concentrating sparsity at the sites identified as profitable in the execution analysis.

% Hand-edited; Run048 observations/001-causal-hz-latency.md;
% results/hz-latency.json, generated by 07_reduce.py.
\textbf{Exploiting the induced zeros contributes directly to this operating point's latency reduction.}
In a separate controlled session on the same $T_2/P_h$ checkpoint at $\kappa=0.1$, disabling both $h,z$ skipping paths raises latency from 0.559 to 0.642\,ms: exploiting these zeros reduces full-model latency by \textbf{$12.9\%$}. Thresholding remains active, and all four execution configurations produce identical $h,z$ activations and validation logits. Disabling $h$ alone adds $89.7\,\mu$s, whereas disabling $z$ alone adds $4.2\,\mu$s. These conditional effects are not additive; the benefit at this operating point comes predominantly from $h$ (Appendix~\ref{app:t2-hz-control}).


\subsection{Results at 70M}
\label{sec:quality-sparsity-results}
% Combined figure: Analysis034/observations/002-combined-scales.md; 02_combined.py.
% Claim audit: Analysis034/observations/003-manuscript-integration.md.
% Matched implementations: Analysis028/observations/001-matched-70m-grid.md.

\textbf{Increasing model size lowers loss but raises latency.}
Figure~\ref{fig:70m-quality-latency} compares the 14M and 70M recipes: native Base loss falls from 5.209 to 4.100, while latency rises from 0.655 to 1.611\,ms. As at 14M, aggressive interventions trade quality for lower latency. For example, $T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ at $\kappa=0.5$ increases loss above Base by 1.116 at 70M, versus 0.621 at 14M. Yet $T_2/P_h$ retains the lowest loss among the three threshold topologies at every matched $\kappa$. At 70M and $\kappa\leq0.1$, it reaches 14.63--15.21\% model-wide sparsity against a 15.44\% ceiling, with loss 4.129--4.193. This concentration of sparsity at $h,z$, with a smaller quality penalty, is consistent with their susceptibility extending across the two scales.

\textbf{Threshold strength controls the quality--latency trade-off at both scales.}
For $T_2/P_h$, increasing $\kappa$ from 0 to 0.1 reduces latency from 0.612 to 0.573\,ms at 14M (0.039\,ms), and from 2.504 to 1.956\,ms at 70M (0.549\,ms). Increasing $\kappa$ further to 0.5 brings 70M latency to 1.214\,ms (a further 0.741\,ms reduction) but raises loss from 4.182 to 4.838. The large gap between $\kappa\in\{0.1,0.5\}$ motivates further testing of intermediate thresholds at $h,z$. The corresponding latency reduction from $\kappa=0.1$ to 0.5 at 14M is 0.067\,ms, so the absolute reduction at 70M is $11.0\times$ as large. This is consistent with exploitable sparse structure at both scales, although it compares separately trained checkpoints and different kernel implementations. Table~\ref{tab:endpoints-14m-70m} in the appendix provides the full comparison across recipes and thresholds.

\textbf{The 14M kernel does not transfer efficiently to 70M.}
On the 70M Base checkpoint, the original port takes 3.325\,ms versus native PyTorch's 1.611\,ms, an additional 1.714\,ms. A further search over 74 candidate implementations improves sparse $h,z$ execution and dense components, reducing Base latency to 2.732\,ms; the remaining gap is still 1.121\,ms. The fastest optimized recipe, $T_7/P_h$ at $\kappa=0.5$, reaches 1.088\,ms, 32.5\% below native Base, but its loss is 5.337, or 1.237 higher. This is an end-to-end engineering comparison across different checkpoints, not a gain attributable entirely to sparsity. The lower-loss $T_2/P_h$ at $\kappa=0.1$ remains slower than native execution even on the same checkpoint (1.956 versus 1.662\,ms; Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-transfer}).

% Controlled 70M evidence: Analysis028/04_controls.py and data/retained-controls.json.
\textbf{Sparse execution remains useful, but its implementation requires adaptation.}
In a separate session on the same 70M $T_7/P_h$ checkpoint at $\kappa=0.5$, replacing only the specialized $h,z$ path with native operations raises latency from 1.128 to 1.365\,ms: a conditional 0.237\,ms (17.4\%) benefit from the specialized path, including fusion and layout effects. The corresponding skip-disabled control is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-transfer}. Thus, useful sparse structure can persist while kernel efficiency fails to transfer, even within one model family. Our results motivate kernels adapted to model size and workload, rather than assuming that a published implementation transfers unchanged. Training interventions and kernel execution must therefore be co-designed: training should induce sparse structures that the target kernel can exploit at an acceptable quality cost.

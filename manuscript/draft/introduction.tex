\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Activation sparsity can reduce inference cost when specialized kernels exploit
zero activations~\citep{liu2024teal,cetin2026sparser}. Choosing how to create
those zeros requires more than a local sparsity percentage: zeros at different
sites affect different amounts of multiplication work, and skipping that work
can itself add overhead. We ask how pressure, thresholding and their placement
change the quality cost of sparsification, and which of the resulting
opportunities translate into faster execution.

Existing recipes combine these choices in different ways. TEAL applies
post-hoc magnitude thresholding with allocation across projections; ProSparse
combines ReLU, progressive activation pressure and threshold shifting; and
Q-Sparse uses top-$k$ selection at attention and FFN projection
inputs~\citep{liu2024teal,song2024prosparse,wang2024qsparse}.
Their ablations motivate a matched study of the additional value of pressure
as thresholding and placement change. An intervention that helps one recipe
may add little to another, or incur a different quality cost at a new site.

We pretrain Pythia-family models from scratch to compare interventions in a
controlled architecture family~\citep{biderman2023pythia}. The detailed 14M
study contains 30 conditions across eight recipes and 29 paired comparisons.
\emph{Pressure} encourages small activation magnitudes; \emph{thresholding}
applies specified zeroing maps; and \emph{placement} selects where each acts.
We compare pressure on/off at fixed nonlinearities and broader thresholding
without pressure; pressured four/seven-site comparisons change complete
objectives as well as placement. Model-wide activation sparsity, $\Smodel$,
weights actual zeros by their counted scalar multiplications. Architectural
reach, $\Rarch$, describes the work reached if the selected sites were entirely
zero; it is not a target guaranteed attainable at useful quality.

The comparisons show that pressure's marginal effect depends on the threshold,
and that local FFN sparsity can mis-rank model-wide opportunity. At the largest
14M threshold, adding orthogonal pressure to seven-site thresholding raises
$\Smodel$ from $15.39\%$ to $27.48\%$, against $29.95\%$ architectural reach,
for $+0.1265$ loss relative to the unpressured recipe and $+0.6208$ relative
to unmodified A0. Activation distributions and operation counts explain why
this recipe has greater model-wide sparsity than its four-site counterpart
despite fewer pooled FFN zeros. We promote selected recipes to 70M and 410M,
where the high-threshold seven-site advantage over four sites persists, while
lower-threshold orderings vary. Trained recipes improve on uniform A0 clipping
at aggressive targets across all three sizes; small quality budgets can
instead favor clipped endpoints.

A human-guided coding-agent case study then tests execution on one RTX5090
with BF16, batch-one, uncached 2,048-token inference and full vocabulary logits.
The selected implementation is $1.234\times$ faster than native PyTorch/SDPA
geometrically across all 30 14M checkpoints. Sparse paths add $1.043\times$
over matched fusion with skipping disabled, helping 14 checkpoints; disabling
attention skipping improves every checkpoint. Thus the native-relative gain
combines fusion with a conditional benefit from sparse execution. The study
connects the effect of an intervention on quality and counted products to the
instruction granularity and overhead that determine whether exploiting its
zeros is worthwhile.

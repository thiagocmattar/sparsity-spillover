\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our results suggest that sparsification should be understood as a joint training-and-deployment problem rather than as a property of activation distributions alone. First, \textbf{local pressure does not imply a local response}: applying pressure only at the FFN hidden site can alter sparsity at other sites, showing that the network can reorganize its internal representations in response to a localized training intervention. More broadly, \textbf{sparsity is useful only if the resulting zero structure can be exploited by the inference system}. $S_{\mathrm{model}}$ is best viewed as a training-time proxy for computational opportunity, not as a direct predictor of latency. In the controlled 14M measurements, the largest latency gains come from $(h,z)$ sites, which were the ones more susceptible to sparsification, with higher achieved sparsity. At 70M, the 14M implementation required further optimization; the specialized $h,z$ path then reduces latency by $17.4\%$ relative to native $h,z$ operations on the same checkpoint, while dense-kernel improvements also contribute to the end-to-end gains (Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-transfer}). One hypothesis is that we must achieve a high ($>90\%$) level of site-layer sparsity, so the zeros gets a exploitable tiled structure with higher probability.

Transformers appear sufficiently flexible to adapt their internal representations to architectural and optimization constraints, suggesting that larger gains may come from \textbf{designing training objectives around inference requirements}, rather than first maximizing a generic sparsity metric and only afterward attempting to accelerate it. The same principle may extend beyond activation sparsity: training could be used to shape representations for properties required by weight sparsity, quantization, batched execution, or other hardware-specific constraints. In this view, deployment requirements are not merely constraints imposed after training, but potential signals for deciding what properties the network should learn.

Several limitations define the scope of these conclusions and point to concrete directions for further work. Our scaling evidence is limited: although the 410M experiments demonstrate that high model-wide sparsity remains attainable, the fixed token budget leaves 410M in a substantially different optimization regime, so it should be interpreted as a stress test rather than a clean scaling experiment. Our runtime study is also restricted to batch-one, full-sequence inference on an RTX5090; we do not study how sparsity interacts with batched inference, cached decoding, or other hardware.

In addition, the full intervention space is combinatorial, and under the available compute budget we evaluate only a selected set of threshold and pressure placements rather than an exhaustive factorial study. The nonlocal response visible in Figure~\ref{fig:pressure-layer-sparsity} motivates interventions concentrated at $h$ and $z$. The $T_2/P_h$ results at 14M support this direction: they preserve much of the execution benefit while avoiding thresholding at sites whose sparsity is expensive in quality or unprofitable for the current kernel. Whether this balance transfers depends on the implementation as well as the sparse structure. Related extensions include learning or tuning different thresholds $\kappa$ at different sites and directly sparsifying attention probabilities after softmax, which could expose work in $pv$ without relying on $q/k$ zeros to propagate through softmax. More generally, rather than asking where the model can be made sparse, a productive systems-guided approach is to decompose full-model inference into individual operations, identify where latency and removable work are concentrated, determine what structured representation would allow those operations to be skipped efficiently, and then design training-time interventions that encourage precisely that structure.

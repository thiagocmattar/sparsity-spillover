\section{Experimental study}
\label{sec:experiments}

We pretrain Pythia-14M, 70M, and 410M from random initialization~\citep{biderman2023pythia} on MiniPile~\citep{kaddour2023minipile}, using 1.493 billion input tokens per model. We evaluate validation loss and $\Smodel$ over all complete validation sequences. Table~\ref{tab:interventions} defines the thresholding and OL1-pressure recipes and their sparsity ceilings.

Within each size, runs share initialization, data order and training budget.
For $T_4/T_7$, we sweep $\kappa\in\{0,0.01,0.05,0.1,0.5\}$ with
$P_0/P_h/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ at 14M, $P_h/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ at 70M, and
$P_{\mathrm{all}}$ at 410M, using $\lambda=b=1$. All sizes include
$T_0/P_0$ and $T_1/P_0$ controls. At 14M, $T_1/P_1$ uses
$\lambda\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$. Including the historical naive-L1 controls
reported in the appendix, the study contains 40/22/12 conditions at
14M/70M/410M (74 total). Appendix~\ref{app:training-protocol} gives the full protocol.

To compare the interventions with post-hoc thresholding, we apply uniform percentile magnitude-thresholding targets $p=0,0.1,\ldots,0.9$ at $a,m,h,z$ as a TEAL-style baseline~\citep{liu2024teal}. Site--layer thresholds are calibrated separately on ten training blocks.

\textbf{Agentic kernel development.}
To test whether the learned zeros save time, we used GPT-6
Astra~\citep{openaigpt6astra} as the configured coding agent in a human-guided
search for specialized GPU routines. For our representation-learning team,
without specialist GPU-kernel expertise, this made direct execution tests
practical. The agent inspected existing implementations, proposed edits,
compiled candidates and revised them using correctness and timing feedback.
We fixed the workload and numerical tolerances, then froze the implementation
before final-checkpoint benchmarking. The final routines pass the full-validation
checks at 14M and, after a shape-specific port, 70M
(Appendix~\ref{app:kernel-results}). This single development case illustrates
the feasibility of this workflow.

In a matrix product, a zero activation contributes nothing to the output.
Avoiding its multiplication can save arithmetic and avoid reading the
corresponding weights. However, GPUs process groups of products;
isolated zeros need not eliminate a group, and finding which work to skip
also costs time. Our implementation detects empty groups and handles nearly
empty rows using their surviving values, especially at $h$ and $z$. It also
combines adjacent operations to reduce intermediate data movement. These
mechanisms make speedup depend on both the activation pattern and the routines
that consume it.

\textbf{Sparsity and speedup rise together within recipes.}
Figure~\ref{fig:base-model-speedup} shows an approximately linear increase
within each trained threshold/pressure family over the tested grid, with
different slopes across families. At 14M and $\kappa=0.5$, for example,
$T_7/P_{\mathrm{all}}$ is much sparser than $T_4/P_{\mathrm{all}}$
($27.48\%$ versus $12.71\%$), yet slightly slower ($1.38\times$ versus
$1.42\times$ base-model speedup). Thus, a larger $\Smodel$ need not yield a
larger gain: where the zeros occur and whether the kernel can exploit them
matter (Section~\ref{sec:kernel-autoresearch}).

\textbf{Post-hoc clipping does not yield comparable gains in this implementation.}
Every positive clipping target remains below $1\times$ at 14M. At 70M,
aggressive clipping can exceed $1\times$, reaching $1.22\times$, but with
substantial loss degradation (Appendix~\ref{app:clipping-results}).
Here clipping uses separate masking operations around the frozen kernel,
adding activation scans and writes on every forward pass. The resulting zeros
still have to form groups that its skipping rules can use. These costs can
outweigh the avoided products; the measurements do not separately isolate
each cost. We did not optimize a fused clipping implementation, so this
result does not establish a general limitation of post-hoc sparsification.

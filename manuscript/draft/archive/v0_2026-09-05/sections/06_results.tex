\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Expanding pressure is a consequential intervention}
\label{sec:pressure-results}
The local 14M comparison establishes that pressure can improve quality and
sparsity together in this training regime (Figure~\ref{fig:matched}, left).
Relative to the ReLU control, ordinary L1 at $\lambda=1$ reduces loss from
5.2696 to 5.1023 and raises \Rmodel{} from 2.71\% to 3.95\%.
OL1 produces similar local opportunities: its loss advantage over ordinary
L1 at the first three coefficients reverses at $\lambda=1$.
This comparison gives limited support for preferring either complete
optimization recipe at the feed-forward hidden state.

The effect changes when pressure reaches more sites
(Figure~\ref{fig:matched}, middle). With A4 gates fixed, moving from $h$-only
pressure to four-site pressure raises loss at all five thresholds.
At $\kappa=.01$, opportunity is essentially unchanged
(8.5858\% versus 8.5867\%), while loss rises from 5.2045 to 5.4583.
At $\kappa=.5$, the expansion adds 2.49 percentage points of opportunity
and raises loss by 0.3153. Broader pressure therefore has an identifiable
quality cost even when more operations are directly targeted.

\subsection{Attention-operand gates change the pressure response}
\label{sec:attention-results}
Without pressure, adding $q,k,v$ gates raises opportunity increasingly with
threshold (Table~\ref{tab:paired}). At $\kappa=.5$, A7 gains 5.17 percentage
points over A4 with a loss increase of 0.0432. The $\kappa=0$ comparison,
where the added gates are identities, differs by only 0.006 percentage points
and $-0.0021$ loss, exposing the scale of residual numerical variation
between these separately executed controls.

The pressure response differs between the configurations. At $\kappa=.1$,
adding seven-site pressure raises opportunity by 1.37 points with a loss
change of 0.0008; four-site pressure adds 2.44 points with a loss increase of
0.1286. At $\kappa=.5$, seven-site pressure adds 12.10 points with a
0.1265 loss increase, compared with 2.50 points and 0.3783 for four-site
pressure. Low-threshold effects are smaller and mixed.
The largest-threshold pressured A7 endpoint has both lower loss and higher
opportunity than its pressured A4 counterpart. These results support the
combined gate-and-pressure recipe and locate the threshold range in which
its benefit is most pronounced. Attribution to an interaction at a fixed
pressure set requires the additional condition described in
Section~\ref{sec:interventions}.

\subsection{Training extends the evaluated frontier, with quality-dependent gains}
\label{sec:frontier-results}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontiers} compares all main-study training conditions with
the complete uniform-clipping paths. At 14M, the trained conditions offer
better opportunity at several quality levels. At 70M, the selected training
grid reaches larger opportunities, while clipping is stronger near the
unmodified model's quality.

To compare at common quality allowances, Table~\ref{tab:budgets} takes the
largest evaluated opportunity among points whose loss is at most
$\mathcal{L}_{A0}+\delta$. Selection uses the discrete tested grid.
At 14M, training exceeds clipping at each reported
allowance. At 70M, clipping reaches 19.99\% opportunity at $\delta=.25$,
versus 10.07\% for the selected trained grid. At $\delta=1$, training reaches
30.57\%, versus 25.11\% for clipping. Thus the 70M results support a frontier
extension at larger loss allowances; the preferred intervention depends
on the quality requirement.

The tighter $\delta=.05$ allowance permits approximately 5.1\% higher
perplexity. At this allowance, the 14M trained grid reaches 9.34\%
opportunity versus 2.55\% for clipping; at 70M the selected trained
interventions exceed the allowance and the control remains the best eligible
trained point. The $\delta=1$ row permits a $2.72\times$ perplexity increase
and describes a substantially more permissive quality target.

\subsection{Where the added opportunity occurs}
\label{sec:operation-results}
The high-threshold A7 recipe changes the actual attention operands
(Figure~\ref{fig:operations}). At 14M, the $QK^\top$ and $PV$ products
contribute 16.77 percentage points to its 27.48\% model-wide opportunity.
For the corresponding A4 recipe, these products contribute only 0.057
points to a total of 12.71\%. At 70M the attention contribution is
10.42 points for A7 and 0.011 for A4. The operation decomposition therefore
explains the additional opportunity opened by gating $q,k,v$.
At these endpoints, $h$ and $z$ are more than 99.8\% zero at both sizes.
This concentration also motivates a capacity-matched comparison with
statically reduced branches. The endpoint measurements describe where zeros occur; intermediate training
measurements would be needed to explain how those representations develop.

The total opportunity rises from 27.48\% at 14M to 40.60\% at 70M for the
high-threshold A7 recipe. However, its block-level opportunity falls from
91.75\% to 82.15\%. The larger model allocates 49.42\% of counted work to
transformer blocks, compared with 29.95\% at 14M
(Equation~\ref{eq:block-share}). This architectural difference accounts for
the higher model-wide fraction despite lower block sparsity.
The two sizes establish that the selected recipe can produce substantial
opportunity in both architectures; a scaling law would require broader
size, data-budget, and seed coverage.

\subsection{An implementation test bounds the efficiency claim}
\label{sec:runtime-results}
We evaluated six 70M checkpoints with a sparse linear path adapted from
\citet{cetin2026sparser} on an H100 NVL. The implementation preserves signed
nonzero values, passes shape-level correctness checks, and matches the
corresponding dense BF16 validation loss within $10^{-3}$. Its full-model
scope replaces downstream linear projections and retains dense attention.
An official sparse-model positive control achieves a $1.288\times$ speedup.

For our checkpoints, every tested full-model sparse configuration is slower
than its dense reference at batch sizes 1 and 32 with $T=2048$.
The best median ratio, dense latency divided by sparse latency, is
$0.983\times$ for A4+OL1@4 at $\kappa=.5$ and batch size 1.
Separate unfused sparse-attention compositions also remain slower.
Appendix~\ref{app:runtime} gives all six results, correctness coverage,
and implementation limits. The learned zeros expose multiplication
opportunity, while execution with this implementation remains slower.

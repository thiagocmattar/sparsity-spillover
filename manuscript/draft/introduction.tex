\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Activation sparsity can reduce language-model inference cost when kernels
exploit zero operands~\citep{liu2024teal,cetin2026sparser}. But a researcher
choosing an intervention under a quality and execution budget faces three
different questions: how many activations become zero, how much computation
consumes those zeros, and how much time an implementation actually saves.
These quantities need not favor the same recipe.

Existing methods combine different mechanisms: TEAL thresholds activations
after training, ProSparse applies activation pressure during continued
training, and Q-Sparse sparsifies attention and FFN projection
inputs~\citep{liu2024teal,song2024prosparse,wang2024qsparse}. Their different
models, training protocols and execution settings make mechanism attribution
difficult. We study \emph{pressure}, \emph{thresholding}, and \emph{site
placement} under a common pretraining protocol. Our contribution is a
controlled comparison connecting their conditional effects to activation
distributions, zero-operand products, and measured latency, rather than a
claim that combining more interventions uniformly improves efficiency.

We pretrain Pythia-family models from scratch for controlled comparisons
across sizes~\citep{biderman2023pythia}. Thirty 14M conditions provide local
and multisite ablations; twelve conditions at each of 70M and 410M evaluate
selected complete recipes independently. We define model-wide sparsity
$\Smodel$ as the fraction of counted scalar matrix multiplications with a
zero activation operand, and selected-site reach $\Smax$ as the fraction
reachable if the selected sites were entirely zero. The latter depends on
architecture and the inference workload, and need not bound natural zeros
outside the selected sites.

The comparisons reveal three disagreements. First, pressure's value changes
with its surrounding recipe. At the largest tested threshold, adding OL1
to the seven-site recipe increases $\Smodel$ from 15.39\% to 27.48\% for
$+0.1265$ loss; the complete recipe costs $+0.6208$ relative to the dense
reference (Figure~\ref{fig:quality-sparsity-overview}). Second, seven-site
OL1 has fewer zeros at the FFN-up input but greater model-wide sparsity
than four-site OL1. Third, that greater model-wide sparsity does not yield
the lowest optimized latency in the tested implementation. These
disagreements expose distinct choices about quality, computational coverage,
and exploitable zero structure.

The $\kappa=0.5$ quality--sparsity ordering recurs across sizes, but the full
threshold response does not. These single-seed comparisons establish
conditional recipe relationships, not a quality scaling law. A human-guided
coding-agent loop supports kernel implementation~\citep{ouyang2025kernelbench,luo2026agentic};
matched ablations separate projection benefit, attention overhead and
native-reference cost.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Activation sparsity can improve language-model inference efficiency when specialized kernels skip computations associated with zero activations. Recent work has shown that sparsity can be exposed in pretrained models through post-hoc thresholding or induced during training, in both cases with measured inference speedups~\citep{liu2024teal,cetin2026sparser}. These results raise two practical design questions: where should zeros be exposed and, independently, where should sparsity pressure be applied? Which resulting zero structure can kernels exploit?

Existing approaches induce activation sparsity in different ways. TEAL applies post-hoc thresholding to pretrained models, ProSparse uses L1 regularization on feed-forward (FFN) activations during training, and Q-Sparse applies top-k sparsification to both FFN and attention activations~\citep{liu2024teal,song2024prosparse,wang2024qsparse}. While each approach is effective in its own setting, it remains unclear how they compare under a common setup and how their effects interact when combined.% An intervention that works well in isolation may add little on top of another or incur a different quality cost when applied at additional sites, making sparsity alone insufficient to identify which choices drive the improvement.

To address this gap, we pretrain Pythia-family models from scratch~\citep{biderman2023pythia} to study the effects of (i) activation $\ell_1$ pressure ($P$) and (ii) \emph{thresholding} ($T$). We focus on Pythia-14M and use Pythia-70M as a scale check. While pressure adds a gradient component to induce smaller activation magnitudes, thresholding maps small values into exact zeros. Each one of them can be placed in different activation sites, giving a combinatorial design set of sparse interventions. For each combination of $P$ and $T$, we derive an architecture-dependent maximum achievable sparsity ($\Smax$), and measure model-wide attained activation sparsity ($\Smodel$) and the final inference latency under a specialized sparse kernel. Figure~\ref{fig:quality-sparsity-overview} presents sparsity vs. quality trade-offs and inference latency for Pythia-14M, across $P/T$ combinations and post-hoc clipping of baselines.

Across 40 14M-parameter and 22 70M-parameter training and evaluation runs, we identify several insights.
First, \textbf{the $T/P$ train-time interventions extend the attainable sparsity range relative to post-hoc clipping}, approaching the architectural ceiling for the more aggressive recipes, but at a cost in quality (Figure~\ref{fig:quality-sparsity-14m-70m}).
Second, \textbf{sparsity and speedup increase together within recipes}, but a larger $\Smodel$ does not directly translate into a larger speedup. The main requirement for lower latency is the location and structure of zeros and whether the kernel can exploit them, with most gains coming from skipped computation at $h$ and $z$ (Table~\ref{tab:kernel-mechanisms}).
% Scale-check evidence: Analysis024 observation 044 and Run040 observation 001.
Third, \textbf{sparsity remains exploitable across the tested scales}: skipping computations can reduce latency at both 14M and 70M, but a sparse kernel does not transfer directly and requires adaptation to the larger model.
Fourth, \textbf{local pressure at $h$ provides a more favorable balance between quality, sparsity and latency than global pressure} when combined with broader thresholding. It lowers loss in all 16 matched comparisons at moderate thresholds, and latency in 18 of 20 across the full grid (Figure~\ref{fig:paired-intervention-effects}).
Finally, \textbf{local pressure can induce global changes in sparsity}: at 14M and $\kappa=0.05$, pressure at $h$ increases sparsity at $z$, which receives no direct pressure (Figure~\ref{fig:pressure-layer-sparsity}).
Taken together, these results indicate that activation sparsification should not be treated as an isolated training objective: training interventions and deployment kernels must be co-designed so that the induced sparsity is both high and structurally exploitable at inference time.
